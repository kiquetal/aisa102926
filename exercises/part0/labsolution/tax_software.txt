## 1. Clarify the functional requirements

### A. User identity and account management

The description says users can file returns for themselves and other family members, but it does not define the identity and account model.

Questions to resolve:

Critical distinction: An account holder, a taxpayer, a return preparer, and a dependent are different concepts. They should not automatically be treated as the same person.

### B. Tax filing scenarios

The requirements identify individual, joint, and dependent filing, but the applicable rules need further clarification.

### C. Federal and state returns

Multi-state filing introduces substantial complexity beyond simply submitting one federal and one state return.

Questions include:

- Which states and territories are supported?

The system also needs a defined way to handle differences between federal and state tax calculations, deductions, deadlines, and eligibility rules.

Two especially important issues are:

1. Submission status is not binary. A return may be prepared, validated, queued, transmitted, received, rejected, accepted, or awaiting further action. The exact states depend on the filing interface and jurisdiction.

The requirements should also specify what happens when a tax authority is unavailable, returns an ambiguous response, or receives a submission for which the application never receives an acknowledgment.

### Regulatory questions

The requirements should identify applicable obligations, subject to legal review, including:

- IRS requirements for tax return preparers, electronic filing providers, and taxpayer information safeguards.
- Federal and state privacy and data protection requirements.
- Applicable tax information confidentiality and retention requirements.

The exact requirements depend on the business model, jurisdictions, services offered, and how data is processed.

## 6. Special scenarios and failure conditions

The requirements should explicitly address exceptional cases, particularly where mistakes could cause financial or legal consequences.

Duplicate or uncertain submissions

A timeout occurs after transmitting a return. The application cannot tell whether the authority received it. How does it avoid accidentally submitting the same return twice?

Rejected returns

The authority rejects a state return because of invalid information. Can the user correct and resubmit it without unnecessarily rebuilding the entire return?

Family and dependent conflicts

Two users claim the same dependent, or a family member revokes permission while a return is being prepared.

Payment failures

A fee payment fails, succeeds but its confirmation is delayed, or is refunded after the return has been submitted.

Deadline and service outages

A tax authority is unavailable close to a filing deadline. What can the application communicate, and what recovery or escalation options are available?

Notification failures

An email bounces or a message is delayed. Can the taxpayer still see the authoritative filing status securely inside the application?

A further issue is concurrent editing. If two authorized people modify the same return, the application must prevent one person's changes from silently overwriting the other's.

Yes, several critical details are missing. I've grouped them by how much they could change the project, and they're from my general knowledge of US tax e-filing, so verify current specifics with the IRS and state agencies.

## 1. Regulatory standing (biggest gap)

You can't simply "communicate with the tax authorities." Federal and state e-filing requires formal authorization, and the requirements don't appear anywhere in your description.

- **IRS authorization:** Software that transmits returns generally must go through the IRS e-file Provider process (an e-file application, EFIN, and software developer/transmitter registration for Modernized e-File). Each tax year requires passing the IRS's Assurance Testing System (ATS), which usually happens in the fall before the season opens. With the 2026 returns opening in January 2027, it's worth checking whether your timeline is realistic or whether you should target the following season.
- **Build vs. partner:** Decide whether you'll be your own transmitter or use a third-party transmitter or tax-engine vendor. This is the biggest cost and schedule lever.
- **State enrollment:** Each state that supports e-file has its own enrollment, schema, and testing, and states differ widely. Some have no income tax, and some have local income taxes (NYC, Ohio and Pennsylvania municipalities). Some states have no free/direct e-file route or require paper for certain forms.
- **Consent and privacy law:** IRC §7216 (consent to use or disclose return information, which affects marketing and analytics), the FTC Safeguards Rule (a written information security program), IRS Publications 1345 and 4557, and state breach-notification and privacy laws.
- **Your role:** Is this self-prepared filing only, or will you offer human review or paid preparer services? That changes which rules apply (PTIN, state preparer registration such as California's CTEC, Circular 230, and so on).

## 2. Tax scope and rules

- **Tax year and annual maintenance:** Which years are supported? Tax law, forms, and schemas change every year, so you need a versioned rules engine and a yearly update process.
- **Supported situations:** The forms, schedules, income types, and credits in and out of scope. Examples are W-2, 1099 variants, self-employment (Schedule C), investments and crypto, rental income, K-1s, EITC and child credits, and itemized vs. standard deductions.
- **Multi-state logic:** Resident, part-year, and nonresident returns, apportionment of income across states, state reciprocity agreements, credits for taxes paid to other states, and community property states.
- **Taxpayer categories:** ITIN holders, nonresident aliens (1040-NR), military, expats, and US territories (Puerto Rico has its own system). Many Spanish-speaking users may be ITIN filers, so this matters for your target audience.
- **Other filing needs:** Amended returns, extensions, estimated taxes, prior-year returns, and paper-file fallbacks when e-file isn't possible.
- **Payments:** Balance due (direct debit, IRS Direct Pay, installment agreements) and state-by-state differences.
- **Post-filing:** Handling rejects and error codes, IRS and state notices, identity-protection PINs, and audit support.

## 3. Family and multi-person filing

This is under-specified and has real legal implications.

- **Joint returns:** Both spouses must consent and e-sign (Form 8879), and each needs identity verification. How will you handle spouses in different locations, or separation and divorce?
- **Dependents and minors:** Who signs for a child's return? What are the rules when a dependent files their own return while being claimed on someone else's?
- **Filing for others:** Authorization to file for adult relatives, deceased taxpayers (Form 1310), or people who are incapacitated. A shared account also raises privacy questions: who can see what?
- **Account model:** One account with multiple returns, delegated access, and how this interacts with each person's identity verification.

## 4. Security, privacy, and fraud

Because you'll hold SSNs, income data, and bank details, this is a top-tier concern.

- Encryption, tokenization of SSNs, MFA, role-based access for support staff, and audit logs.
- Identity proofing at signup, plus fraud controls for stolen-identity refund fraud and for changes to bank account details.
- Data retention and deletion policy (balancing record-keeping requirements against minimizing sensitive data).
- Breach response plan and notification obligations, plus compliance targets such as SOC 2.
- Whether bank details are stored at all, or collected only transiently for the return.

## 5. Payments and fees

- **Card payments:** PCI DSS (ideally via a processor so you never touch card data).
- **Bank accounts:** ACH/NACHA authorization rules and routing/account validation.
- **Fee structure:** Pricing per federal and state return, any free tier, refund policy, chargebacks, and sales tax on software in some states.
- **Fee deduction from refunds:** This usually requires a bank partner and brings extra regulatory requirements, so decide early whether you want it.

## 6. Language and accessibility

- Spanish support needs more than UI translation. IRS and state e-filed forms are in English, so you need clear handling of that, accurate tax terminology, Spanish-language support staff, and Spanish legal disclosures and consent text.
- Accessibility (WCAG/ADA) and mobile-first design.
- Whether more languages will be added later (affects your i18n architecture).

## 7. Notifications

- Email/SMS consent (TCPA, CAN-SPAM) and opt-out handling.
- Never put sensitive data in messages. Use "log in to see an update" patterns.
- Status sources: the IRS acknowledges receipt, accepted, or rejected, but refund tracking and state status vary, and many states provide no API.
- Notification rules for rejects, deadlines, and disaster-related extensions.

## 8. Non-functional and operational

- Extreme seasonal load (January through April, with spikes at the deadline) and uptime requirements during deadlines.
- Customer support staffing and tooling.
- Data import options (W-2 OCR, payroll providers, prior-year returns) and accuracy validation.
- Testing: a regression suite for calculations that proves correctness across thousands of scenarios.

## 9. Business and liability

- Accuracy guarantee, audit-defense policy, and who is liable for errors.
- Terms of service, disclaimers, and E&O insurance.
- If you plan to use AI for guidance, how it's constrained and disclosed.
